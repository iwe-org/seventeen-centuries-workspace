# Virtue

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius takes virtue as the one settled good: justice, truth, temperance and fortitude, with nothing better to be found; its motion is "something more divine" than that of the elements, it lies "not in passivity, but in activity", and "in justice the other virtues have their foundation". Machiavelli keeps the word but breaks its tie to success: "something which looks like virtue, if followed, would be his ruin", it is unnecessary to have the good qualities and "very necessary to appear to have them", and Hannibal's "other virtues were not sufficient" without cruelty — though he still refuses to call Agathocles' crimes talent. Nietzsche makes virtue relative to rank and to the one who has it: "The virtues of the common man would perhaps mean vice and weakness in a philosopher", "Happiness and virtue are no arguments", "One is punished best for one's virtues", and a species simply names as virtues the qualities by which it has survived.
