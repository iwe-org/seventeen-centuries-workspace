# Philosophy

## Summary

Nietzsche argues that in every philosophy there comes a point where the philosopher's personal conviction appears on the scene, revealing the autobiographical nature of philosophical systems. He laments that modern philosophy has degraded itself by submitting to scientific authority, reduced to mere theory of knowledge that timidly refuses to cross any threshold. Against this decline, Nietzsche invokes the royal magnificence of ancient philosophical anchorites like Heraclitus and Plato as the standard by which contemporary philosophers appear contemptible. Nietzsche portrays the philosopher as fundamentally different from the scholar or scientific man, arguing that every great philosophy is an involuntary autobiography revealing its creator's moral impulses. The genuine philosopher lives in necessary contradiction to his age, serving as its bad conscience by putting the vivisector's knife to contemporary virtues. Philosophers of the past distinguished between exoteric and esoteric teachings, viewing things from above rather than below, and the true philosopher remains essentially a recluse whose writings conceal as much as they reveal.
