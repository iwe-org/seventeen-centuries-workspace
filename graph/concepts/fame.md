# Fame and Glory

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius argues fame away: it is "a thing devoid of judgement", "after-fame is oblivion", the "longest posthumous fame" is short because those who remember "will very soon die", praise from the many is "a clapping of tongues", and nothing beautiful is made better "by being praised". Machiavelli makes reputation a working asset of the prince, who should "in every action" seek the "reputation of being a great and remarkable man"; renown protects against conspiracy, a "reputation for being mean" is worth bearing, and glory remains a standard above success — Agathocles' crimes "may gain empire, but not glory". Nietzsche is closer to Marcus in suspicion but psychological in method: praise is obtrusive, "I listened for the echo and I heard only praise", vanity is something a noble man can hardly understand, and the reverence paid to great men rests on success, "the greatest liar".
