# Wisdom

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius says little about wisdom by name and folds it into right living: a man near death does not "yet place wisdom only in acting justly", nothing "is more agreeable than wisdom itself", prudence stands with temperance, justice and fortitude among the real goods, and the knowing soul is the one that knows "the reason which pervades all substance". In Machiavelli wisdom is prudence, a ruler's practical foresight: evils foreseen early, "which it is only given to a wise man to see", can be cured; "prudence consists in knowing how to distinguish the character of troubles"; "a wise lord cannot, nor ought he to, keep faith" to his own harm; and "a prince who is not wise himself will never take good advice". Nietzsche turns against exactly that prudential sense: the moralities of prudence are "expediency, expediency, expediency", wisdom "seems to the populace to be a kind of flight", and the genuine philosopher lives "unwisely" and "imprudently" — though he grants a wisdom in men's being superficial.
