# Pity and Sympathy

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius allows pity a narrow, cool place: when a man wrongs you, see what he took for good and evil and "thou wilt pity him, and wilt neither wonder nor be angry"; men's ignorance can "move our pity". For himself he refuses it — labour not "as one who would be pitied or admired". Machiavelli weighs mercy by its results: a prince who makes a few examples is "more merciful" than those who "through too much mercy, allow disorders to arise", and he need only appear merciful. Nietzsche, whose translator writes "sympathy" for pity, makes it a central target. He hears in the modern "religion of sympathy" the herd's wish to abolish suffering, says "Pity has an almost ludicrous effect on a man of knowledge", and warns "Not to cleave to a sympathy" — while keeping a sympathy of his own, which he counts among the virtues one must master.
