# Anger

## Summary

Anger is one of Marcus Aurelius's standing problems, and nearly every passage is a remedy for it. The offender is ignorant and a kinsman — "nor can I be angry with my kinsman" — and merely follows what seems good to him, so the response is to show him his error; "Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink?"; the gods "are not vexed" at tolerating such men; anger hurts more than the acts that provoke it, and "to be moved by passion is not manly". Machiavelli has no passage on it. Nietzsche distrusts not anger but its moral dress: he speaks of "the stupidity of moral indignation" as the sign that humour has left a philosopher, and holds that "no one is such a" liar as "the indignant man"; yet he does not share Marcus's ideal of mildness, since the man whose sympathy is worth something is one "who has his indignation and his sword".
