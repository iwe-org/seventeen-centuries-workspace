# The People

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius meets the people only as a source of opinion to be discounted: "the praise which comes from the many is a clapping of tongues", Socrates called their opinions "bugbears to frighten children", and Antoninus is praised for not "flattering the populace". Machiavelli takes them as the ground a prince stands on: "it is necessary for a prince to have the people friendly", which is easy because "they only ask not to be oppressed", and "the best possible fortress is—not to be hated by the people"; but "the nature of the people is variable", and "the vulgar are always taken by what a thing seems to be". Nietzsche, like Marcus, writes from above them, but with open contempt: "Where the populace eat and drink, and even where they reverence, it is accustomed to stink", this is "the age of the masses", and "the instinct of the populace cries" for freedom from all masters — though he finds more tact for reverence among peasants than among the educated.
