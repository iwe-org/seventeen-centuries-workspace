# Gratitude

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius opens his book as an act of gratitude — "To the gods I am indebted" for parents, teachers and friends — and wants to die "from thy heart thankful to the gods", like the ripe olive thanking its tree; but he asks none for himself: a benefactor should be like a vine that seeks nothing after bearing grapes, and when he blames a man as ungrateful the fault is his own. Machiavelli reasons from the opposite assumption, that men are "ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly, covetous": obligation is a weak link, new benefits do not cancel old injuries, yet men are bound by the benefits they confer as much as by those they receive, a fact a prince can use. Nietzsche makes gratitude a mark of rank — the "irrestrainable stream" of it in Greek religion shows a very superior kind of man, and prolonged gratitude, like prolonged revenge, belongs to the noble among equals — while warning that even gratitude needs caution.
