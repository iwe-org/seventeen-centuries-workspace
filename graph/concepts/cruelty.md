# Cruelty

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius speaks of cruelty mainly through its opposite: he praises his father's "mildness of temper" and that there was "nothing harsh" in him, holds that "mildness and gentleness" are "more manly" than passion, and warns not to feel towards "the inhuman" as they feel towards men; the one thing he calls cruel is forbidding men to strive after what seems suitable to them. Machiavelli weighs cruelty as a tool: there are "severities being badly or properly used", Cesare Borgia's "cruelty reconciled the Romagna", a prince "ought not to mind the reproach of cruelty", yet emperors "cruel and rapacious" were destroyed by hatred. Nietzsche makes cruelty a root of culture: there is "a great ladder" of religious cruelty, "One ought to learn anew about" it, knowledge itself has "something cruel" in it, and the noble man "has reverence for all that is severe and hard".
