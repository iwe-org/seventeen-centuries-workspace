# Pride and Vanity

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius knows pride only as a fault to be worn down. He thanks a father "able to take away all pride from me", mocks the conqueror with "A spider is proud when it has caught a fly", asks of all human things seen from above "Are these things to be proud of?", and catches the last refuge: "the pride which is proud of its want of pride is the most intolerable of all". Machiavelli has no passage on it. Nietzsche separates what Marcus runs together. Vanity, the need for others' good opinion, is "one of the things which are perhaps most difficult for a noble man to understand"; pride is the "exalted, proud disposition" by which the noble type knows itself, and the faith that demands the "sacrifice of all freedom, all pride" is its enemy. His aphorisms play the two against each other — "Our vanity is most difficult to wound just when our pride has been wounded" — and he charges the Stoics with pride toward Nature.
