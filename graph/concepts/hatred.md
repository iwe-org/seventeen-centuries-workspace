# Hatred and Contempt

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius refuses hatred altogether: he cannot hate a kinsman made for cooperation, the man who hates his neighbour cuts himself off from the whole like a severed branch, and if another despises or hates him — "Let him look to it" — he will stay mild and benevolent; his own contempt is kept for perishable things, not persons. Machiavelli treats hatred and contempt as the two dangers a prince must avoid above all, a whole chapter being given to it: rapacity towards property and women makes him hated, fickleness and irresolution contemptible, liberality leads to both, and "the best possible fortress is—not to be hated by the people". Nietzsche restores both passions to honour as signs of rank. "One does not hate as long as one disesteems, but only when one esteems equal or superior"; the noble man despises the cowardly and the petty, affability hides too much contempt of men, and the modern preacher of sympathy betrays self-contempt.
