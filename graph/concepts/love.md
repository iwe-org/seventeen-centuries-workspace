# Love

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius makes love a duty of the rational and social animal: "Love mankind", love the men among whom one's lot is cast "truly, sincerely", and — what is peculiar to man — love even those who do wrong, since they are kinsmen who err unwillingly; he extends the word to fate itself, loving only what is spun with one's destiny. Machiavelli asks what love is worth to a ruler and answers that "it is much safer to be feared than loved", because love hangs on a link of obligation that men break at every opportunity; still a prince does well to be beloved by the people and must above all avoid hatred. Nietzsche takes love apart psychologically: one loves one's desires rather than the thing desired, love between the sexes is a refined will to possess, and love of the neighbour is secondary to fear of him. Yet what is done out of love takes place beyond good and evil, and love as a passion is of noble origin.
