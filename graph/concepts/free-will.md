# Free Will

## Summary

Machiavelli keeps free will as a working premise: so as not to extinguish it, he holds that fortune is the arbiter of half our actions and leaves the other half, or a little less, to us. Nietzsche takes the notion apart. Free will is a "hundred-times-refuted theory" that owes its persistence to the charm of being refutable; "freedom of the will" is really the feeling of supremacy of the one who commands and identifies himself with the executor of the command; and the wish for it in the superlative metaphysical sense is the wish to be a causa sui, to bear the whole responsibility for one's actions and absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance and society. "Free will" and "non-free will" are both mythology: in real life there are only strong and weak wills. He turns the same critique on theology, where God's free will is refuted by his failure to make himself understood. Marcus Aurelius has no passage linked here.
