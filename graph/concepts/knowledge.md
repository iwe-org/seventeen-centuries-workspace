# Knowledge

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius subordinates knowledge to conduct: he bids himself "cast away the thirst after books", is glad not to have wasted time on syllogisms or the heavens, and holds that the only knowledge that matters is of the reason that governs the whole and of good and evil, ignorance of which is why men do wrong; one who despairs of dialectic can still be free and social. Machiavelli values one kind only, practical and historical: his dearest possession is "the knowledge of the actions of great men", and a prince should study war, learn his country's terrain and read histories. Nietzsche makes knowledge itself the problem. The will to knowledge rests on a more powerful will to ignorance, "Knowledge for its own sake" is "the last snare laid by morality", physics is interpretation, the scientific man is an instrument rather than an end, and the seeker of knowledge is an artist of cruelty against his own inclinations — measured by how much truth he can endure.
