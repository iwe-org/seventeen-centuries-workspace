# Obedience and Command

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius speaks of obedience almost only upward and inward: to reason, to the gods, to one's own daemon. For him "the law is master, and he who breaks the law is a runaway"; a man placed by a commander should stay at his post; and no one can make rules for others "before thou shalt have first learned to obey rules thyself". Machiavelli asks how a prince gets obeyed: "it is not reasonable that he who is armed should yield obedience willingly to him who is unarmed", in adversity citizens "are not of a mind to obey him", and Scipio's forbearance ruined his army's discipline. Nietzsche makes obeying and commanding the structure of will, morality and society alike. "Thou must obey someone, and for a long time" is nature's moral imperative, yet in the European herd obedience has been bred "at the cost of the art of command", which he wants restored.
