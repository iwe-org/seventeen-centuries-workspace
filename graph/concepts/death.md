# Death

## Summary

Death is Marcus Aurelius' constant exercise and nearly absent from the other two. He strips it to "an operation of nature", "a mystery of nature" like generation, "a dissolution of the elements", and argues that the longest liver and the soonest to die "lose just the same"; dying is "one of the acts of life", to be neither despised nor feared, and one should "Depart then satisfied". Machiavelli never reflects on death as such; it appears only as a political fact — subjects offer to die for a prince "when death is far distant", mercenaries will not die for a stipend, men "more quickly forget the death of their father than the loss of their patrimony", and an assassin "who does not fear to die" cannot be guarded against. Nietzsche gives it two aphorisms: one should part from life "blessing it rather than in love with it", and the thought of suicide "is a great consolation".
