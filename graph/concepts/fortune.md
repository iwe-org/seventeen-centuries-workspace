# Fortune

## Summary

Machiavelli makes fortune a named adversary of political skill: principalities are won "by fortune or by ability", those raised by luck keep their place with difficulty, and though fortune is "the arbiter of one-half of our actions" she can be dammed like a river or mastered by boldness, so princes should blame "their own sloth" rather than her. Marcus Aurelius barely grants fortune independent standing: what comes from it is "not separated from nature" and Providence, and whether the world is providence or a "fortuitous concurrence of things" the response is the same — blame neither, and "if chance rules, do not thou also be governed by it"; good fortune is simply a good disposition of the soul. Nietzsche uses chance rather than fortune: history has been a "frightful rule of folly and chance" that new philosophers must end, and for the higher man "Happy chances are necessary", and he must seize them.
