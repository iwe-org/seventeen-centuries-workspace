# Courage

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius moralises courage: it is holding one's post and not fearing nature. He who is "grieved or angry or afraid" is a runaway, and it is mildness, not anger, that is manly — the gentle man "possesses strength, nerves and courage". Machiavelli wants courage visible and useful: a prince should "show in his actions greatness, courage, gravity, and fortitude", mercenaries are worthless because "valiant before friends, cowardly before enemies", and against fortune "it is better to be adventurous than cautious". His translator's "valour" often means ability as much as bravery. Nietzsche moves courage into thought. He files conventional morality under "Morals as Timidity", hears in it "the timidity of the herd", and asks of thinkers the courage to own their conclusions: "The great epochs of our life are at the points when we gain courage to rebaptize our badness as the best in us"; courage heads his "four virtues".
