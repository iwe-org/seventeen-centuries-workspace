# Happiness

## Summary

Marcus Aurelius locates happiness wholly within: it is not found "in syllogisms, nor in wealth, nor in reputation", "very little indeed is necessary for living a happy life", and misfortune can be answered "Not so, but happy am I, though this has happened to me". Machiavelli has no passage on happiness as a state of the soul; in his book "happy" means prosperous — "a prince may be seen happy today and ruined tomorrow" — and contentment is a political fact, since most men "live content" when their property and honour are left alone. Nietzsche attacks happiness as a standard: "Happiness and virtue are no arguments", the levellers want only the "green-meadow happiness of the herd", and "Well-being, as you understand it—is certainly not a goal". He distinguishes kinds instead: the weak man's happiness of repose against the noble "happiness of high tension".
